# Facilitation playbook — running the discussion, scoring the decision

Loaded at step 6 of the workflow. The A-case is written and sealed; the learner has read
it and knows nothing of the outcome. Your job for the next stretch is to be the section
instructor, not the answer key.

## The opening

Open on the decision, not the company. HBS sections open with a question that forces a
position within the first minute — "You are <protagonist>. It is <date>. What do you do?"
Not "what did you find interesting about this case?", which invites summary.

Then make them **commit verbally before they analyse**. An early stated position is
something to defend and revise; an analysis-first learner will hedge until they have
reverse-engineered what you want to hear.

## The moves

| Move | When | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold call** | The learner is summarising rather than deciding | "Pick one. You can change your mind in five minutes, but pick one now." |
| **Disagreement pump** | A position is stated too easily | "The strongest argument against you is ___. Answer it." Supply the counter yourself if the learner can't. **Ask for one or two, never a long list** — see the caution below. |
| **Evidence audit** | A claim leans on a number | "Where in the case is that? What's the citation?" |
| **Constraint tightening** | The plan does everything | "You have one quarter and one engineer. What survives?" |
| **Comparator turn** | Confidence is high | "The comparator did roughly this and did not survive. What's different about you?" |
| **Protagonist reality check** | The advice is abstract | "You have to say this to your board on Monday. Say it in three sentences." |
| **Uncertainty naming** | Late in the discussion | "What do you not know that you'd most want to know? What would you pay to learn it?" |

### Caution: the disagreement pump can backfire if you over-pump

Asking someone to argue against an outcome's inevitability is the standard hindsight
remedy, and it **attenuates rather than eliminates** the bias. It also has a documented
failure mode: pushed to generate *many* alternatives, people experience the effort as
difficult, read that difficulty as evidence that few alternatives existed, and end up
**more** hindsight-biased than if you had never asked. Two strong counters beat ten weak
ones. When the learner starts straining, stop asking and move on — the additional
objections are not just worthless, they are actively counterproductive. Evidence in
[case-method-evidence.md](case-method-evidence.md).

## Two things you must not do

- **Do not steer toward the historical decision.** You know it; they don't. The historical
  choice is one option, and in a well-chosen case it is often *not* the best available one.
  Steering converts a decision exercise into a guessing game about your intent.
- **Do not leak through framing.** Tone, ordering and the option you spend most time on
  all carry signal. If you find yourself dwelling on the option that won, redistribute.

## Handling "just tell me the answer"

Say the true thing once, plainly: the outcome exists and is sealed, and it will be
revealed the moment a decision is committed — the ordering is the point, and it is a
five-minute delay, not a refusal. Then offer the shortcut that is legitimate: if they are
stuck on a **fact** (what did the market cost structure look like? was the regulation in
force yet?), give it. Withholding data is not the same as withholding the outcome, and
conflating them just makes the case artificially hard.

If they still want the story rather than the exercise, give them the story — but name the
trade: they are buying a narrative and forgoing the practice, and the narrative is the
part most contaminated by hindsight.

## Scoring after the reveal — process, not outcome

Score the committed decision on the four things that were knowable at the time. A correct
call reached by luck scores poorly; a wrong call reached well scores respectably, and
saying so out loud is most of the lesson.

| Dimension | Question | Poor | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | Did they decide the right question? | Answered a narrower or adjacent question | Named the real trade-off and why it was the crux |
| **Evidence use** | Did they use what was on the table? | Asserted from priors; ignored cited facts | Anchored each claim to the case record |
| **Uncertainty** | Did they see what was unknowable? | Treated a coin flip as determined | Named the live uncertainties and sized them |
| **Falsifiability** | Did they name what would flip them? | "I'd adapt as needed" | A specific observation with a threshold |

Then attribute the historical outcome across three buckets out loud: **decision quality**,
**execution**, and **luck**. Most admired outcomes have a larger third bucket than the
retelling admits, and forcing the split is the antidote to the pattern-extraction step
that follows.

## The debrief question set

This is where the retrospective analysis belongs — after the commit, after the reveal,
after the process score. These are the pattern-extraction questions from the popular
prompt, kept in substance and placed where they cannot contaminate the decision:

1. What were the critical decisions, and which of them were non-obvious **at the time**
   (check this against what the A-case said was uncertain, not against the outcome)?
2. What did they refuse to do under pressure, and what did that cost them?
3. What broke as they scaled, and was the fix structural or a patch that held long enough?
4. **What pattern here would transfer** — and does the comparator that failed falsify it?
   If the failed comparator did the same thing, the pattern is not the cause.
5. What would you do differently running it today, given what has changed since?
6. Close: *"The single most transferable lesson from <company> for someone building
   <learner's business> is ___"* — stated as a **testable claim**, with the observation
   that would show it false in the learner's own context.

Question 4 is the one that does the work. Without it the debrief is the original prompt
again, just later in the session.
