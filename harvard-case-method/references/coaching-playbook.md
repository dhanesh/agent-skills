# Coaching playbook — running the discussion without doing the deciding

Loaded at steps 4–7. The brief is assembled and lint-clean. Your job now is to make the
user's reasoning better without replacing it.

## The opening

Open on the decision, not the topic. "It is <date>. You have to choose. Which option, and
why?" — a position stated early is something to defend and revise; a user who analyses
first will hedge until they've reverse-engineered what you want to hear.

If they haven't got a decision yet — "our pricing is a mess" — the first job is framing,
not analysis. A decision has a **verb and a date**. Push once, then work with whatever
they give you.

## Widening the option set (step 4)

This is the highest-leverage moment in the whole session, because the option set is what
silently caps decision quality. Your three options in the brief are a floor, not a menu.
Ask for a fourth they generate. The four that get missed most:

| Missed option | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **Buy information first** | "What would you pay to know X before committing? Is there a cheap version of this decision that just buys that?" |
| **Sequence it** | "Does this have to be one decision? What's the version you do in two steps, with a checkpoint?" |
| **Reversible variant** | "What's the version you can undo in a month? What does it cost you to make it reversible?" |
| **Change the frame** | "Is the real decision one level up — not *which pricing*, but *whether pricing is the constraint*?" |

If every alternative they list is a variant of one idea, say so plainly: that is an option
set of one wearing three hats.

## The moves

| Move | When | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Commit early** | They're summarising, not choosing | "Pick one now. You can change it in ten minutes." |
| **Steelman the counter** | A position lands too easily | "The strongest case against you is ___. Answer it." **One or two, never a list.** |
| **Evidence audit** | A claim leans on a number | "Where's that from? Is it in the brief with a citation?" |
| **Outside view first** | An estimate arrives before a base rate | "Before you estimate — what happened to the two reference cases?" |
| **Values surfacing** | Two options look tied | "They're only tied if retention and margin matter equally. Rank them." |
| **Constraint tightening** | The plan does everything | "One quarter, one engineer. What survives?" |
| **Reality check** | The reasoning is abstract | "Say this to your board in three sentences." |
| **Uncertainty pricing** | Late | "What don't you know that you'd most want to? What would you pay for it?" |

### The over-pumping caution

Steelmanning is a consider-the-opposite move: it **attenuates** bias rather than
eliminating it, and it has a documented backfire. Pushed to generate *many* alternatives,
people find the effort hard, read the difficulty as evidence that few alternatives exist,
and end up **more** biased than if you'd never asked. Two strong counters beat ten weak
ones. When they start straining, stop — the extra objections are worse than worthless.

## The premortem (step 7)

Script it precisely, because the wording carries the effect:

> "It is <resolution date>. This decision failed — not 'might have', did. Tell me the
> story of how it happened."

Then hold them to **fact, not possibility**. "Procurement might read it as a price rise"
is a hedge; "Procurement read it as a price rise, and three anchors renegotiated down" is
the premortem. Certainty is the active ingredient — that's what produces the ~30% lift in
reasons identified, and roughly twice as many action-shaped ones.

Ask what, if anything, the premortem changes about the decision. Often it changes nothing
and adds a falsifier — that is a fine outcome, and the premortem still goes in the record.

## Writing forecasts they can actually be scored on (step 8)

Most first-draft forecasts are unresolvable. Fix them at commit time, never at resolution
time.

| Bad | Why | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "The launch goes well" | Unresolvable | "≥2 of 3 Q3 renewals close on the hybrid" |
| "0.5 — could go either way" | 0.5 on everything is a refusal to forecast | Force a lean, even 0.45 or 0.55 |
| "0.95, I'm confident" | Over-confidence is the default failure | "What would have to be true for the 1-in-20 case?" |
| "By next year" | No date | A specific resolution date |

Push back on all four. A probability of exactly 0 or 1 is rejected by the tool: certainty
is not a forecast.

## The debrief (step 9)

Three things, in order, and nothing else:

1. **The weakest link**, named — frame, alternatives, information, values, reasoning, or
   commitment — and one sentence on why. Quality is the minimum, so one link is the
   answer, not six scores.
2. **One thing to do differently** next time. Concrete and small.
3. **What is not yet known**: whether the decision was right. Nobody knows. Say it.

Never grade the decision by an outcome that hasn't happened, and when outcomes do land,
ask "what was knowable at the time?" before "what did you get wrong?" — often the honest
answer is that nothing was knowable and the decision was sound.

## Drill mode, when you use it

Historical cases give same-session feedback that live decisions can't. Two rules:

- **Pick an ending the user doesn't know.** Most famous companies fail this. A company
  that failed, a mid-sized firm in their sector, or a non-famous decision inside a famous
  company all work better than the story everybody has heard.
- **Author and run in separate sessions where you can.** The seal protects a file, not a
  context window: an agent that just researched the ending knows it while facilitating,
  and knowing leaks through emphasis and which option gets the follow-up question. If you
  must do both in one session, say plainly that the commitment is on the honour system.

Drill scoring works the same way — resolve the forecasts against the reveal, and the Brier
score joins the same profile as the live decisions.
